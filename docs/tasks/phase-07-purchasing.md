# Phase 07 — Purchase orders, bills, approvals and three-way match

**15 tasks · 67 points**

The payable side. Structurally the mirror of `Sales`, with two things sales does not have: **documents that arrive from outside** and must be parsed rather than composed, and **approval**, because money leaving needs a second person in a way money arriving does not.

The expensive failure here is paying the same invoice twice, and the dangerous one is paying the right invoice to the wrong account. Both get dedicated tasks.

---

### PF-130 · Purchase requisitions
`Purchasing` · **3 pts** · depends: PF-026 · standards: —

- [x] An internal request before a commitment: requester, cost centre, justification, estimated value. One step further back than an order - the business has not even chosen who to buy from - so it posts nothing, and a check asserts that raising one writes no journal entry
- [x] **The justification is required and the database insists on it.** Strip it out and a requisition is a purchase order with extra clicks: what makes the step worth having is that somebody wrote down why and somebody else read it, which is the only thing a budget holder can act on three months later when asked why the department spent what it did. "Needed" is what somebody types to get past a required field, so `purchase_requisitions_justification_says_something` refuses it
- [x] The cost centre is a **dimension**, not a column of its own, so it is the thing that carries forward to the order and then to the posting. A `cost_centre` column would be a fifth dimension the ledger does not know about
- [x] The prices are named `estimated_unit_price` and `estimated_amount` throughout, because the supplier has not quoted. A column called `unit_price` reads as a price somebody agreed, which is how an estimate ends up on a purchase order unchanged and then in a dispute about what was ordered
- [x] Routed for approval by the same engine as bills, so there is one approval mechanism and not two. The engine is already generic over its subject, so this costs nothing and buys the limits, the delegation record, the fingerprint check that returns an edited document to the start of the chain, and the refusal when the requester tries to approve their own request. A workflow of its own would mean a second set of limits to keep in step with the first, and the one that drifts is the one nobody is looking at
- [x] The fingerprint covers the **justification**, deliberately. Changing *why* after somebody approved it is a different request: the budget holder agreed to the reason as much as to the figure. The note is not covered, because it is correspondence rather than substance
- [x] Approval is **read from the chain, not asserted**. `recordApproval()` takes the request, checks it belongs to this requisition and that it is approved, and reads the final approver from the decisions. A service that took the caller's word would bypass the limits, the delegation record and the duty check in one call
- [x] Optional per entity - a small tenant should not be forced through a requisition step. `purchasing.require_requisition` defaults to off, because a three-person business forced through it approves its own requests to itself all day and learns to click past them, and an approval somebody has learned to click past is worse than none: in an audit it looks like a control. Where it is on, **`PurchaseOrders::raise()`** refuses an order that names no requisition - which is the only place the rule can be enforced, since the requisition is the document that would otherwise simply not exist
- [x] Approved requisitions convert to purchase orders, carrying their dimensions forward - line first, then the header. The cost centre that approved the spend is the one the expense lands against; re-entering it on the order is how a department ends up carrying a cost another department asked for, and the requisition is the only document that records who asked
- [x] Conversion takes the **quoted** price where one is given and keeps the estimate where it is not - and the order's note says which lines carry an estimate. Refusing would be wrong, because stationery at a catalogue price genuinely needs no re-quoting; copying an estimate in silently would be worse, because somebody will later read it as an agreed price
- [x] One requisition converts once, and the converted requisition names its order (`purchase_requisitions_conversion_names_its_order`). Two orders from one request both look authorised, and "what came of my request" answered by searching the orders for something similar is how the second one gets raised
- [x] A rejection needs a reason, in the service and in a constraint. The requester is going to ask, and "rejected" with nothing behind it is what makes them re-submit the same request with a different title
- [ ] The segregation-of-duties engine adds nothing to a requisition beyond the requester check. `assertTheyMayDecide()` refuses `requested_by` directly, which covers the ordinary case, and then asks the duty engine about `bill.approve` on a subject of type `requisition` - where the object-scoped `bill_create_then_approve` pair finds no matching history because raising a requisition is recorded as `requisition.raised`. Closing it means either a duty pair of its own in PF-033's seeder or a mapping in `config/sod.php`, and the choice belongs with PF-033 rather than here

### PF-131 · Purchase orders
`Purchasing` · **5 pts** · depends: PF-130, PF-064 · standards: —

- [x] Supplier, lines with item, quantity, unit price, tax code, delivery date, dimensions. Tax on an order is **indicative**: a buyer wants to know roughly what it will cost including tax, and the figure is never posted and never claimed, because the authority's figure comes from the supplier's tax invoice and an order's estimate that found its way into a return would be a claim supported by a document that does not exist
- [ ] Sent to the supplier as a PDF, and by Peppol where the supplier is reachable. **Open, and blocked on two things that are not code.** The PDF needs the rendering toolchain PF-102 to PF-104 are blocked on - Gotenberg, Ghostscript and veraPDF are not installable in this environment - and sending by Peppol needs the AS4 transport and SMP lookup of PF-121 and PF-122, which need a network and an accredited access point. The order document itself is a UBL `Order`, which is a different document type from the invoice the Exchange plane serializes today
- [x] A commitment is tracked per line for budget visibility, and is never a financial posting. **There is no journal entry anywhere in `PurchaseOrders`**, and a check asserts it: an order is a promise, not a transaction - nothing has been delivered, nobody is owed anything and no expense has been incurred. Systems that post an "encumbrance" to a ledger account end up with a balance sheet carrying a liability nobody owes, which reconciles to nothing and has to be explained to an auditor every year. The remaining commitment is `(ordered - billed) x price`, computed per line
- [x] And it is released by the **bill**, not by the receipt. A receipt without a bill is money already spent and not yet invoiced, which is the accrual's job - releasing the commitment on delivery would release it before the obligation existed. A check receives the goods and asserts the commitment has not moved
- [x] Partially received and partially billed orders are tracked **per line**, not per order. `quantity_received` and `quantity_billed` are columns on the order line: an order for ten of which six arrived and four were billed is the ordinary case, "60% received" says nothing about which six, and the three-way match has to compare a bill line against the quantity received for *that* line
- [x] Closing an order with uninvoiced receipts is refused with an explanation, and the explanation names the lines and quantities. That quantity has a GRNI balance behind it - a liability for goods the business is holding and has not paid for - and closing the order would strand it in the control account with no open document pointing at it, so the only way to find out what it was would be to read the ledger backwards. "Cannot close" without the figures sends somebody hunting through three documents
- [x] A closed order says **why**, in the service and in a CHECK constraint. An order closed with nineteen units never delivered and no reason is what somebody finds in a year while asking what happened to them, and by then the buyer has left. Cancelling an order that has received lines is refused outright: something arrived, and the supplier either keeps it or takes it back - both are events that need recording

### PF-133 · Bills
`Purchasing` · **5 pts** · depends: PF-082, PF-047 · standards: EN 16931 · `type:posting` `type:schema`

- [x] Supplier invoice with lines, tax breakdown, the supplier's own document number and date, and the derived due date
- [x] **Three dates, and they are not interchangeable.** The supplier's document date is theirs and is what their statement says; the tax point is the jurisdiction's and decides which return the input tax falls in; the posting date is ours and decides the period. A bill dated the 28th of March that arrives on the 3rd of April belongs to the supplier's March and our April, and a system with one date gets two of the three wrong. `Bill` deliberately has **no** `date()` accessor: a method that picked one would be read by whoever came next as "the date of the bill"
- [x] A tax point after the posting date is refused, because it would claim input tax in a return that has not opened. The other direction is normal and is the reason the two dates exist
- [x] The payment terms are frozen and **counted from the supplier's date**, not ours. "Thirty days" means thirty days from their invoice date, which is what they will chase on - counting from our posting date would quietly extend every bill that arrived late, and the supplier would be right
- [x] Posts accounts payable, input tax, and expense or asset - per **line**, because one bill routinely carries a repair and a machine and a document-level choice forces the business to split a supplier's invoice for an accounting reason
- [x] With the **reverse-charge case posting both input and output tax**, which is the half most systems forget. The supplier charges nothing and the recipient accounts for both sides: an output because they owe it, an input because they may claim it. A system that posts only the input side files a return whose input box is right and whose output box understates the liability by exactly that amount - and because the two net to nothing in the accounts, nothing looks wrong until the authority compares the return against the supplier's own declaration
- [x] And reverse-charge tax is **not part of the payable**. The supplier charged nothing: they are owed the net. A gross that included it would overpay them by the tax and leave the authority's side unpaid
- [x] Non-recoverable input tax posts to expense rather than to the input account, keeping the input account reconcilable to the return - and to the **line's own** expense account, because the cost of a purchase includes the tax that cannot be recovered and it belongs to the cost centre that bought it. Recovery is a percentage rather than a flag, because partial recovery is the normal case for entertainment, fuel and mixed-use assets
- [x] The rate group is keyed by category, rate **and** the reverse-charge flag: the same rate under the reverse charge is a different box on the return, and merging the two would make the return's two sides indistinguishable
- [x] Found on the way: `journal_lines` refuses a tax point with no tax code, which is right - a tax point that cannot be attributed to a code is a figure in a return nobody can trace - so the bill's rate group carries the code, and the posting passes both or neither
- [ ] A bill in a foreign currency books at the rate on its tax point date - the columns are there and the rate is not yet fetched; PF-068's rate service is wired on the sales side and this call site arrives with PF-138, which needs it for the approval limit test as well

### PF-135 · Three-way match
`Purchasing` · **8 pts** · depends: PF-133, PF-132 · standards: — · `risk:high`

- [x] Bill against purchase order against receipt, on **quantity and price**, per line. Price as well as quantity because a bill for the right quantity at the wrong price passes a quantity-only match, and that is the common form of supplier overcharging: the units are right and the rate crept up. Only an increase is an exception - a supplier billing less than agreed is their mistake, and raising one would hold a payment the business owes
- [x] Tolerances per supplier and per line type, in both absolute and percentage terms, with a platform ceiling. Both, because neither alone works: two per cent of a fifty-unit order is one unit and two per cent of a fifty-thousand-unit order is a theft, while a flat tolerance of one unit flags every bulk delivery that arrived a kilogram light. The **larger** of the two is allowed - the percentage carries scale and the absolute carries the small orders where a percentage rounds to nothing. Where nothing is configured the answer is zero, because a tolerance nobody configured is a tolerance nobody decided on, and the safe reading of "nobody decided" is "ask a person"
- [x] The ceiling is a CHECK constraint, not a service rule. Ten per cent is the most the platform permits: a tenant who sets fifty has turned the match off, and the thing a three-way match exists for is the invoice that is quietly twenty per cent too high. A limit enforced only in a service is a limit an import does not have
- [x] A mismatch routes to an exception queue **naming the specific discrepancy** - "billed 120 units, received 100" - rather than a generic hold. The figures are columns of their own as well as prose, so the queue is ordered by how far out each one is: a queue ordered by date puts a two-cent rounding difference above a thousand-unit over-billing, and whoever works it has to open every one to find out which matters
- [x] Eight kinds, each answering a different question: no order (where the entity requires one), nothing received, billed beyond received, billed beyond ordered, priced above the order, the order already closed, the wrong supplier, and a service nobody confirmed. A service bill with no confirmation is its own kind rather than "received 0", which would read as a delivery that never came
- [x] Found on the way: billing 101 against an order of 100 that was fully received raised **both** quantity kinds with identical figures, because received and ordered were the same number - two rows for one problem, which doubles the queue and makes somebody answer the same question twice. `quantity_over_ordered` is now gated on the excess having actually arrived, which is the case the receipt comparison cannot express. The duplicate pair had also hidden a defect: a check comparing the first two rows of the queue found the pair in both positions and passed with the ordering removed
- [x] And found with it: the queue's own query had no `ORDER BY` beneath the sort, so PostgreSQL was free to return the rows in a different order between runs on the same data - which makes a paginated queue skip rows and repeat others
- [x] Matching handles partial receipts, partial bills, and one bill spanning several orders. The running `quantity_billed` is on the **order line**, which is what lets a second bill be caught: the first consumed the receipt and the second has nothing left to match. A bill-level counter could not tell the second bill how much of the order the first one used, and the duplicate detector cannot see this case at all because the numbers differ
- [x] A matched bill records what it matched against, so the match is auditable afterwards. `bill_lines.purchase_order_line_id` and `goods_receipt_line_id` are written by the match and kept, so "was this bill ever checked against anything" has an answer six months later and the answer is not "somebody says so"
- [x] The match runs **twice**, and only the second run consumes. At entry, to tell the person typing what is wrong while they still have the supplier's document in front of them; at posting, which is the run that advances the order line. A draft bill that advanced the counter and was then abandoned would leave a commitment released against a bill that never existed, and an order nobody could receive against again
- [x] An open exception refuses the posting, and the refusal **names the figures** rather than counting them: the person who posts is frequently not the person who can answer, and the message is what they will forward. A match exception cleared by posting through it is a control that exists only on paper
- [x] Found on the way: the exceptions have to be raised **outside** the transaction the refusal rolls back. Raising them inside it would hand the caller a message and keep no record of it, which is the one failure mode an exception queue cannot have - so the checking run is outside and the consuming run is inside, where advancing the order line has to succeed or fail with the posting
- [ ] The receive-then-approve pair is **not** expressed as a segregation-of-duties rule. Whoever confirms a delivery should not approve the bill for it - the fictitious-receipt fraud is to confirm a delivery that never came and approve the invoice for it - but the duties sit on two different objects linked by an order, and PF-033's scopes are the same document, the same counterparty or the same period. Closing it means a chain scope in the SoD engine that expands a bill into the receipts behind it, which is a change to PF-033 rather than to this task

### PF-141 · Inbound Peppol bills
`Purchasing` · **3 pts** · depends: PF-122, PF-133 · standards: Peppol BIS Billing 3.0

- [x] Documents received by the `Exchange` plane become draft bills with every field already populated and nothing guessed. `UblReader` reads a document into the **semantic model** - which is the standard's own model and not a document of ours - and a round-trip check serializes a real invoice, reads it back, and asserts the same business terms come out and no others. A term the serializer emits and the reader does not read is a field an inbound bill arrives without; a term the reader produces that the document does not carry is a guess wearing a business term's name
- [x] Nothing is defaulted in for a missing element. A document with no `cbc:DocumentCurrencyCode` yields no BT-5, the cardinality rules say so, and it is rejected with the rule number - a reader that filled in "probably SAR" turns a supplier's mistake into our own, and the first sign of it is a payment in the wrong currency
- [x] Prefixes are not namespaces. Every expression matches on `local-name()`, because a sender may use any prefix they like - `ns2:Invoice` is as valid as `Invoice`, and several large finance systems emit `ns0:` throughout - and a reader bound to the prefixes we happen to write reads our own documents and nobody else's. A check re-prefixes a document and asserts it reads identically
- [x] An Invoice and a CreditNote are different documents, in the reader as in the serializer: `cac:InvoiceLine` against `cac:CreditNoteLine`, `cbc:InvoicedQuantity` against `cbc:CreditedQuantity`, `cbc:InvoiceTypeCode` against `cbc:CreditNoteTypeCode`. A reader that knew only the first reads every credit note as having nothing on it
- [x] The inbound document is **validated before anything of ours is written**, and a failing document is rejected rather than half-imported. The bill is created in one transaction after every stage passes, so there is no state in which half a supplier's invoice is in our ledger - a failed import that leaves a draft bill nobody raised is then approved by somebody who assumes a draft came from a colleague
- [x] A failing document is rejected with **the standard's own response**: a UBL `ApplicationResponse` carrying the UNCL 4343 code and one `cac:LineResponse` per violation, naming the rule and its explanation. The sender's system raised the document and the sender's system is what has to be told; an email reaches a person who then has to describe a BR rule number to a developer. A rejection with no report is refused by the database, because "rejected" is a verdict and a report is a reason
- [x] An unmatched document answers `CA` - conditionally accepted - rather than `AP`. Saying accepted would tell the sender their invoice is in our ledger when it is in a queue
- [x] A document carrying a DOCTYPE is refused before anything is read, and bytes that are not XML are rejected with a report naming the parser's own message. Entity expansion needs no external reference: ten nested entities expand a few hundred bytes to a gigabyte, and the parser is doing exactly what the specification says
- [x] The original XML is archived as received, under a kind of its own, **before it is parsed** - and the same bytes delivered twice are one row, because an access point retries and an email is forwarded twice. Without that, the second delivery becomes a second draft bill: the duplicate-payment error arriving through a door the duplicate detector does not watch, since it only sees bills
- [x] Supplier matching by electronic address, falling back to tax registration, then to a sender allowlist, then to a human decision - **never to a name guess**. Two suppliers called "Gulf Trading" are two suppliers, a Latin transliteration of an Arabic name has at least four plausible spellings, and a fuzzy match would be right most of the time: "most of the time" on a payment instruction is not a standard anybody would defend out loud. The `match_method` column enumerates what is allowed, so the absence stays absent even if somebody later adds a helpful-looking function
- [x] The electronic address is matched with its **scheme**, because the scheme is half of the identifier: the same digits are a company number in one registry and a VAT number in another. And a hand-assigned supplier is recorded as a human decision with the evidence, because a match nobody can explain is a match nobody can defend
- [x] Found on the way: **BT-31 carries a country prefix and our stored identifier does not.** BR-CO-9 requires the prefix and the builder adds one, so a document says `SA310000000000003` while `party_tax_registrations` holds `310000000000003` - which is what the authority issued and what somebody reads off a certificate. Comparing only the document's form matched nothing at all, so every inbound document went to a person; the matcher now tries both forms rather than changing what the tax engine, the returns and the printed invoice all read
- [x] And found with it: **EN 16931 has no per-line tax amount.** A line carries its category and rate and the tax lives in the VAT breakdown per rate group, so each line's tax is derived and then reconciled to the amount the document states for its group, any remainder landing on the group's largest line. Three lines of 1.03 at 15% derive 0.4635 where the group states 0.46, and a bill built line by line would carry that difference away from a document that was correct
- [x] The expense account comes from the supplier's configured default or from nowhere. EN 16931 has no term for it - the chart is ours, not theirs - so guessing from the item description would be a guess, and a draft with no account cannot post: `BillPostedRule` refuses a line without one rather than choosing, because letting a posting rule choose is how a purchase ends up in a suspense account nobody reconciles
- [ ] The AS4 transport and SMP lookup that would deliver a document here. This task's work is what happens **after** delivery, and all of it is built and checked; receiving over the network is PF-121 and PF-122, which need an accredited access point and a certificate

### PF-142 · Document extraction
`Purchasing` · **5 pts** · depends: PF-140 · standards: —

- [x] Embedded XML extracted where present - a Factur-X PDF is a structured document and **must never be sent to OCR**. The routing decision is a method of its own (`routeFor()`) so it can be checked without an engine, and the check uses a reader that records being called: asserting on the proposal's source would pass even if the engine had been called and its answer thrown away, and the cost of the call is the point - it is a guess where a fact was available
- [x] `EmbeddedXml` walks the PDF's streams for an embedded invoice, by subtype then by the conventional file names, and handles stored and `FlateDecode` streams. It does **not** render, lay out or interpret anything: a PDF whose structure it cannot follow returns null and the caller falls back, because the alternative is a half-read stream passed to an XML parser as though it were a document. A stream under any other filter is refused rather than guessed at - `/Crypt` means the file is encrypted and `/DCTDecode` means somebody has labelled a JPEG as an invoice
- [x] And an inflated stream is size-capped before it is allocated. A stream of a few hundred bytes can claim to inflate to a gigabyte, and this reads a file a stranger sent
- [x] Found on the way, and the sharpest of them: **a stream ends where its `/Length` says, not where a newline is.** Trimming a trailing end-of-line instead ate a real byte whenever the stream's own last byte was a carriage return - the chunk then ends CR LF, a `\r\n` strip removes both, the deflate stream is one byte short, and it inflates to nothing. The document reads as a PDF with no invoice in it and goes to OCR: the exact failure this task is about, on a file that was perfectly valid. Roughly one compressed stream in 256 ends with that byte
- [x] And `endstream` contains `stream`, so a search for the next stream can land inside it and read the keyword that follows as data. There is a check with a deliberately malformed file, because "malformed" is a shape the sender controls
- [ ] **OCR as the fallback.** The pipeline is built and the engine is not: no OCR engine is installable in this environment, so `extraction.driver` defaults to `none` and `UnavailableOpticalReader` **refuses** rather than returning nothing. "Nothing could be read from this document" and "no engine is installed" look identical in a queue and mean opposite things - the first sends somebody to re-scan a legible invoice - so a proposal records the absence with the reason. There is deliberately no stub returning plausible figures: a fake reading that looks real is how a suite comes to assert the behaviour of something that does not exist, and the first real engine would have to match a fiction
- [x] Everything **around** the engine is built and checked, which is where the risk is. The checks that need a reading use a stated fake, declared as one
- [x] A reading **proposes rather than asserts**. `extraction_proposals` is called that because that is what it holds: an extracted figure is a machine's interpretation of a picture, and recording it as the document's amount makes a guess indistinguishable from a fact two screens later. The bill is built from the confirmed values, never the proposed ones
- [x] Per-field confidence, with fields below the threshold flagged for review - and **the flag is stored rather than derived**. A threshold that changed would move it, and a reviewer who confirmed a field that is now flagged would look careless rather than consistent
- [x] **A confidence belongs to a guess, and a trigger enforces it both ways.** Embedded XML carries none, because the figures were never in doubt and a 1.0 beside a fact is how a reviewer learns to ignore the column; an OCR field must carry one, because a reading of a picture stored as a plain value is a guess nobody can tell from a fact. A trigger rather than a CHECK, because the source is on the proposal and the confidence is on the field
- [x] No extracted bill posts without a human confirming it, enforced by `extraction_proposals_a_bill_was_confirmed_by_somebody`: a proposal that names a bill has a confirmation with a person and a time behind it. The point of an extraction pipeline is to save typing, not to remove the decision - a machine reading that posted itself is a payment nobody authorised
- [x] The confirmation is audited, and a correction is kept **beside** what was proposed rather than overwriting it. Overwriting loses the fact that it was misread, which is the input the accuracy figure needs
- [x] Extraction accuracy is tracked per supplier template, worst first, so a consistently misread format is visible - it is a format to fix, by asking the supplier for Peppol or by teaching the engine, and a list ordered by name hides it among the ones that read perfectly. The counters live on the template rather than being computed from the fields, because a confirmed proposal's fields are frequently pruned and a derived rate would fall to zero as they went - reading as an engine that improved
- [x] A second reading replaces the first rather than joining it. Two proposals would be two sets of figures with no answer to which a person confirmed

### PF-143 · Expense claims
`Purchasing` · **5 pts** · depends: PF-136 · standards: — · `type:posting`

- [x] Employee claims with lines, receipts attached, per-diem and mileage rules, and a policy check
- [x] **Per-diem and mileage rates are frozen onto the line**, from the policy in force on the date the expense was incurred. A rate changes annually in most jurisdictions, and a claim for last November re-derived at today's rate pays the wrong amount and cannot be reconciled to what was approved - the same reasoning as a bill's payment terms, for the same reason. A per-unit claim with no rate in force is refused, because a per-unit claim without a rate is a figure somebody typed
- [x] A policy carries a rate, a ceiling, or both, because the categories differ: mileage has a rate and no ceiling, a meal has a ceiling and no rate, a hotel has both in some businesses. A table that forced one shape on all of them would make the others carry a column that means nothing, and a column that means nothing is a column somebody fills in
- [x] **A policy breach is flagged with its figures, not refused.** A hotel over the ceiling because the conference hotel was the only one left is a real claim, and refusing it sends somebody to a spreadsheet and an email. But an unexplained breach does not reach an approver: the approver is the person the policy exists to inform, and a flag with no text is a line somebody approves without knowing what is wrong with it
- [x] Posts to a payable **to the employee**, which is a distinct control account from trade payables. An employee is not a supplier: the balance is reported differently in most frameworks, it is frequently settled through payroll rather than through the payment run, and an aging report that mixed the two would show a colleague's taxi fare beside a supplier whose terms are thirty days. It matters for the fraud this module is about as well - "who do we owe money to" with employees mixed into it is a list nobody reads closely, and an expense claim is the cheapest place in a business to take money out of it
- [x] And the claimant is a **user**, not a party, so the posting carries no party id. Modelling an employee as a supplier puts a colleague in the list every party-scoped control reads - the supplier bank-detail rule included, which is the highest-value fraud in the product
- [x] Input tax recovered only where a valid receipt is attached **and** the category permits it **and** a policy was in force on the day. Three conditions, and they fail differently: no receipt is no evidence, client entertainment is no entitlement however good the evidence, and a category nobody has written a policy for is not a category somebody decided tax could be claimed on - the safe reading of "nobody decided" on a tax return is "do not claim"
- [x] The decision and its reason are **stored on the line**, and a CHECK constraint refuses recovery without both a receipt and a stated reason. "Why was this claimed" has to be answerable a year later, when the policy has changed and the return has been filed, and re-deriving it would answer with today's policy - which is not the question
- [x] Non-recoverable tax rides with the line's own expense rather than reaching the input account. Tax sitting there that will never be claimed is tax that ties to no box on any form, and the difference is found during an audit by somebody who did not put it there
- [x] Approval through the same engine; a claimant cannot approve their own claim. The fingerprint covers the policy explanations, because an approver agreed to a breach on the strength of a sentence and changing the sentence afterwards is a different claim. And `claim_then_approve` is no longer a declared SoD gap: `expense_claim.submitted` and `expense_claim.approved` are both recorded, so the duty pair can fire for somebody who entered a colleague's claim and then approved it
- [x] What is owed to employees is reportable per person, which is what makes `employee_payable` reconcilable and is the reason it is a control account of its own: "what do we owe our own people" is a question somebody asks at a month end, and it cannot be answered from a trade payables balance with colleagues mixed into it
- [ ] Reimbursement joins a payment run like any other payable. The claim's balance sits in a control account a payment run can read, and the payment run itself is PF-145 to PF-152 in Phase 09 - so this is a dependency rather than a gap in this task
